Water Access

On the Water, or Looking at It?

Condo listings use "waterfront," "bayfront," and "water view" loosely. Before you fall for a balcony, figure out which of these you're actually looking at.

Waterfront buildings

The building's land meets the water: a bay, river, channel, or canal, often with a seawall. In a condominium, the shoreline, seawall, and any docks are usually common elements the association maintains with owners' money, so their condition shows up in the budget and reserve study, not just the view. The declaration of condominium says what's common and what's yours.

Water-view buildings

The water is in front of you, but something sits in between: a road, a park, or another property. Bayshore Boulevard is the classic Tampa example: the towers there look across the boulevard and its waterfront sidewalk to Hillsborough Bay, with no shoreline or docks of their own. A great view, and a different thing from waterfront.

Bayfront or Gulf-front

Bayfront buildings face Tampa Bay, Hillsborough Bay, Old Tampa Bay, Boca Ciega Bay, or Sarasota Bay, usually with calmer water. Gulf-front buildings sit on the barrier islands facing open water and the beach, with more exposure to wind, salt, waves, and storm surge. That exposure shows up in maintenance, insurance, and the building's long-term repair plan. Our waterfront buying guide compares the settings in more detail.

Buildings with boating facilities

Some waterfront condominiums have their own marina, slips, lifts, or dry storage. Many, including some directly on the water, have none. If a boat is part of the plan, treat the slip as its own piece of due diligence, because a slip "available" in the listing can mean very different things.

Deeded, Assigned, or Leased: What a Boat Slip Really Means

The words in the listing matter less than the documents behind them. Three common arrangements:

Deeded

The slip is described as property you own, sometimes as its own unit or parcel. It may be sold with the condo or separately, and it may carry its own fees, taxes, and rules.

Assigned

The association assigns the use of a slip, often as a limited common element tied to a unit or through a board-managed list. Whether it transfers to you depends on the declaration and how it was assigned.

Leased or licensed

You rent the slip from the association or a marina operator for a set term. Availability, waitlists, rates, and renewal follow the agreement, not the sale of the unit.

For any slip, ask for the recorded documents or agreement that create it, whether it transfers at closing, any waitlist, and the limits on boat length, beam, and lifts. Then confirm the water depth at low tide and the route to open water, including any fixed bridges. Every right and restriction must be confirmed in the property and association documents for that specific building.

Where to Look

Where Tampa Bay's Waterfront Condos Are

Each part of the bay offers a different trade-off between walkability, open-water views, boating, and storm exposure. Here's how they differ, so you can decide where to focus your search.

River & Channel

Downtown Tampa, Harbour Island & the Channel District

High-rise and mid-rise living along the Hillsborough River and the channels around downtown, close to the Riverwalk and downtown's offices and venues. Harbour Island is a small island community across a short bridge from downtown. The water out front is often a river or channel rather than open bay.

Check: what the unit actually faces, and how much of the view could change if nearby lots are developed.

Island Neighborhood

Davis Islands

A residential island neighborhood just south of downtown, with a yacht club, marina facilities, and a boating culture of its own. Condos and townhomes sit alongside single-family homes, so building sizes and ages vary widely.

Check: elevation and flood history for the specific building. More on Davis Islands and South Tampa

Water View

Bayshore Boulevard & South Tampa

Condo towers and mid-rises along Bayshore look across the boulevard and its waterfront sidewalk to Hillsborough Bay, minutes from Hyde Park and SoHo. These are water-view buildings: no private shoreline, and no slips out back.

Check: floor height and orientation, which drive the view. Explore South Tampa neighborhoods

Old Tampa Bay

Westshore & Rocky Point

Rocky Point is a narrow strip along Old Tampa Bay near the Courtney Campbell Causeway, where hotels, offices, and condos share the shoreline. Nearby Westshore puts you close to the airport and the business district, which suits buyers who travel often.

Check: storm exposure and evacuation routes along the causeway corridor.

Urban Bayfront

Downtown St. Petersburg

Towers and mid-rises face Tampa Bay within walking distance of waterfront parks, the Pier, museums, and restaurants. Most downtown housing is condominium living, which makes comparing buildings as important as comparing units.

Check: building age and where it stands on inspections and reserves. St. Petersburg area guide

Gulf-front & Intracoastal

The Pinellas Gulf Beaches

From St. Pete Beach and Treasure Island to Indian Rocks Beach and Clearwater Beach, condos sit either on the Gulf or on the calmer intracoastal side. Each beach town is its own city, so rental and building rules change from one bridge to the next.

Check: what the 2024 hurricanes damaged and how it was repaired. Compare the Gulf beaches

Bayfront & Islands

Sarasota

Bayfront towers downtown and on Golden Gate Point, and Gulf-front condos on Longboat Key, Lido Key, and Siesta Key. Longboat Key spans two counties, so the county affects taxes and services.

Check: island access and bridge traffic against the beach. Sarasota area guide

Florida Condominium Due Diligence

Florida's Condo Rules, Explained for Buyers

Since 2022, Florida has added inspection, reserve, and disclosure requirements for many condominium buildings. They give buyers far better information than before, if you know what to ask for and when.

Statute references checked against the 2026 Florida Statutes on October 20, 2026. These laws change often; confirm current requirements for your purchase.

Milestone structural inspections

Florida Statutes §553.899

Condominium and cooperative buildings three habitable stories or more need a milestone inspection by December 31 of the year the building turns 30, then every 10 years. The local building official may require the first one at 25 years, for example for buildings near salt water.

A licensed architect or engineer does a visual phase one. If it finds substantial structural deterioration, a more detailed phase two follows, and the law sets timelines for starting repairs. The association must send owners the inspector's summary within 45 days of receiving the report.

Ask: Is the building required to have one? Was there a phase two? Which repairs were recommended, and are they finished, scheduled, or unfunded?

Structural integrity reserve study (SIRS)

Florida Statutes §718.112(2)(f)–(g)

Residential condominiums with buildings three habitable stories or higher must have a reserve study at least every 10 years. It covers the roof, structure, fireproofing and fire protection, plumbing, electrical, waterproofing and exterior painting, windows and exterior doors, and other items costing more than $25,000 (or a higher inflation-adjusted amount the state publishes each year) whose failure would affect those systems.

For budgets adopted on or after December 31, 2024, these associations generally can't waive or reduce reserves for those items. A limited, owner-approved pause is allowed after a milestone inspection to pay for recommended repairs.

Ask: When was the study done, and by whom? Does this year's budget fund what it recommends? Has the association paused or reduced contributions?

Budget, reserves & special assessments

Florida Statutes §718.116

The budget shows what your monthly fee pays for and how much goes into reserves. Compare it with the reserve study's funding plan. A gap is often closed later with higher fees or a special assessment.

A new owner is jointly and severally liable with the previous owner for assessments that came due before closing. That's why the association's estoppel certificate, which itemizes amounts owed and amounts scheduled to come due, matters before you close.

Ask: Are any special assessments adopted, being paid, or under discussion? How much remains, and when is it due?

Association loans & lines of credit

Florida Statutes §718.112(2)(f)2.c

Associations may fund required reserves through regular assessments, special assessments, lines of credit, or loans, with the approval of a majority of the total voting interests for anything other than regular assessments. A loan spreads the cost out, but owners still repay it through their assessments. Certain reserve-related lines of credit and loans must also be disclosed in the annual financial statement and to prospective buyers.

Ask: Is there outstanding association debt? What's the balance, the term, and how much of each assessment goes to repaying it?

Board minutes, repairs & litigation

Florida Statutes §718.111(12)

Minutes are official records the association must keep permanently, and associations managing 25 or more units must post approved minutes from the preceding 12 months on an owner-access website or app. They often show what the budget doesn't: repair projects, contractor problems, insurance renewals, rule changes, and disputes.

Ask: the seller for the last 12 months of minutes. Look for unfinished repairs, pending lawsuits or claims involving the building, and talk of future assessments.

Resale disclosures & your 7-day right to cancel

Florida Statutes §718.503(2)

When buying from an owner who isn't the developer, you're entitled, at the seller's expense, to the declaration, articles, bylaws and rules, the annual financial statement and budget, the milestone inspection summary if applicable, the latest reserve study or a statement that none was done, the turnover inspection report where applicable, and the association's FAQ document.

If you didn't receive them more than 7 days before signing, the contract is voidable by written notice within 7 days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays, after you sign and receive them. The right ends at closing and can't be waived. Contracts signed after December 31, 2024 must also state whether the milestone inspection, turnover report, and reserve study are required and complete. Buying new from a developer? Different rules apply under §718.503(1).

Ask: for the full document package at the start, and write down the date you received the last item. That date starts your clock.

Financing & Insurance

Bring in Your Lender and Insurance Agent Early

With a condo, the lender and the insurer look at the building as well as you. Finding a problem at the end of your review period is stressful. Finding it in the first few days gives you options.

Financing

Lenders Review the Building, Too

A mortgage on a condo depends on the project as well as your credit. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, for example, treats a project as ineligible when it needs critical repairs affecting safety, soundness, structural integrity, or habitability, when a special assessment for critical repairs is unresolved, or when certain litigation over safety or structural soundness is pending, until those issues are resolved. FHA, VA, and portfolio lenders have their own rules.

  • Send your lender the building name early, before or right after your offer is accepted.
  • Ask what they need from the association, such as a condo questionnaire, and how long it usually takes.
  • Paying cash? Your next buyer may need a loan, so the building's eligibility still affects resale.
Insurance

Know What the Master Policy Leaves to You

Under §718.111(11), the association's property policy covers the condominium property as originally installed, but excludes items such as floor, wall, and ceiling coverings, electrical fixtures, appliances, water heaters, built-in cabinets and countertops, window treatments, and your personal property. Those are yours to insure, typically with a unit-owner policy.

  • Wind: ask what the master policy's hurricane deductible is and how the association would pay it after a storm.
  • Flood: ask whether the building carries flood insurance, and talk with your agent about a policy for your unit. FloodSmart.gov explains condo flood coverage.
  • Quotes: get real quotes during your review period. Costs vary too much by building to estimate.

Florida's Division of Consumer Services has insurance guidance and a helpline. For flood zones, look up the address on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. To plan the rest of your budget, try the property tax and closing cost estimators.

Living There

Questions Worth Asking Before You Move In

Every building writes its own rules. These are the ones that most often surprise waterfront condo buyers after closing.

Fees

What the monthly fee covers (insurance, water, internet, amenities, reserves) and how it has changed over the last few budgets.

Rentals

Minimum lease terms, how many leases are allowed per year, tenant approval, and any amendments being proposed. Beach cities add their own rules.

Pets

Limits on number, size, or type. Requests for assistance animals are governed by fair housing law, not only the association's pet rules.

Parking

Whether spaces are assigned, deeded, or first come, how many come with the unit, guest parking, and whether trailers or golf carts are allowed.

Boat slips

Whether a slip comes with the unit, transfers at closing, or requires a waitlist, plus size and lift limits and marina fees.

Approval & rules

Whether the board must approve the sale or has a right of first refusal, plus rules on renovations, move-ins, and hurricane shutters.

Maintenance

Who repairs and replaces windows, sliding doors, balconies, and air conditioning. The declaration decides, and it varies by building.

Long-term costs

The projects in the reserve study's timeline. Budget for the building's plan over the next decade, not just this year's fee.

Selling a Waterfront Condo

Thinking of Selling? Start With the Paperwork.

Florida requires condo sellers to give buyers the association's key documents, and a buyer's 7-day cancellation window runs from when they receive them. Pulling the package together early, before you list, helps you avoid delays and answer the questions serious buyers and their lenders will ask.

Value is the other half. Two units in the same building can be worth very different amounts depending on the floor, the direction they face, the view, a slip, and the building's finances, and comparable sales are often few.

What's My Waterfront Condo Worth? How Luke Handles Selling

Gather Before You List

  • Governing documents, rules, budget, and financial statement.
  • Milestone inspection summary and reserve study, or the association's statement on each.
  • Special assessments: what's adopted, paid, and proposed.
  • Boat slip and parking documents, if either comes with the unit.
  • Your own records: renovations, permits, and insurance claims.

Most of this comes from the association or its manager, and some may take time to get.
